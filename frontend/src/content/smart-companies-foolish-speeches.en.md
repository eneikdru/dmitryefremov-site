# Smart Companies, Foolish Speeches

## Why technology systems often think better than the people chosen to symbolize them

Sam Altman says OpenAI knows how to build AGI and now discusses a world in which AI performs most intellectual work. Elon Musk promised more than a million autonomous robotaxis by 2020 and now predicts that work will become optional and money largely irrelevant. Jensen Huang declared human language the programming language of the future and later imagined millions of digital agents inside NVIDIA. Bill Gates writes that there is no upper limit to AI intelligence and that progress will not plateau before it exceeds the human level.

These are not four accidental slips. They are examples of the same genre: a person standing next to a functioning technological system begins to speak as if the competence of that system belonged to him personally.

From a scientific and engineering perspective, many of these statements are remarkably weak. Definitions disappear. So do operating conditions, metrics, error costs, falsification criteria and the boundaries of extrapolation. What remains is confident delivery.

I do not need to diagnose the intelligence of these men or invent insulting labels. Their public claims can be examined like any other claims. That is enough. The examination often reveals a standard of reasoning that would not be tolerated from an ordinary engineer.

The billionaire’s reputation, however, follows unusual physics. The weaker the proposition, the more readily it is called vision.

## Altman: knowing how to build what cannot be defined

In January 2025, Sam Altman wrote: “We are now confident we know how to build AGI as we have traditionally understood it.”

As a scientific or engineering statement, this is missing almost everything.

Which definition of AGI is being used? What observable properties must the system possess? What is the evaluation protocol? What result would falsify the claim? Where is the boundary between economically useful automation, further scaling of current methods and the arrival of a genuinely general capability?

“We know how to build AGI” does not transmit verifiable knowledge. It transmits the confidence of the person controlling the company’s public interface.

The contrast with OpenAI’s more formal documents is instructive. They acknowledge uncertainty, gradual transitions and the inability to predict every alignment problem in advance. The engineering organization speaks more carefully than its chief storyteller.

In April 2026, Altman moved further. While discussing society after superintelligence, he described a possible world in which AI performs “most of the intellectual work,” requiring changes to labor taxation, unemployment support and the balance between capital and labor.

That is an accurate account of his argument, not an established forecast. There is no shared unit of measurement connecting current models to “most intellectual work.” There is no deployment model, responsibility model or stable definition of completed work. A direction of extrapolation is presented as a political-economic fact before the object being measured exists.

The problem is not that executives must never speculate. The problem begins when confidence grows faster than knowledge and a commercial hypothesis acquires the status of historical inevitability.

## Musk: a forecast no engineer would be allowed to forget

In 2019, Elon Musk said that “next year for sure” there would be more than a million Tesla robotaxis on the road. Their reliability would supposedly allow occupants to stop watching the road and even sleep.

The deadline passed. The promised result did not arrive.

In a functioning engineering culture, a failed forecast of this scale would trigger a model review. Which assumptions were wrong? What was omitted: perception edge cases, the long tail of road situations, verification, regulation, liability or the operational design domain? How should the forecasting method change?

In the cult of the visionary, the old prediction simply dissolves. A new one appears, and the systematic error receives a flattering name: ambition.

In 2026, the same pattern expanded from a product forecast into a theory of civilization. Musk repeated that within ten to twenty years work would become optional, like gardening for pleasure, while money would lose its relevance thanks to AI and robots.

The robot quietly changes from a difficult capital asset with energy requirements, maintenance, supply chains and an owner into a free force of nature. Property, raw materials, land, energy, political power and claims on machine output disappear. Economics is abolished by a sentence.

The most useful contradiction comes from Tesla’s actual engineering. In the same year, Musk acknowledged that scaling Optimus would be exceptionally difficult because almost everything in the robot was new and no ready supply chain existed. That is a substantive observation. It also undermines the effortless story about imminent universal abundance.

Tesla and SpaceX produce real complex systems because thousands of people calculate loads, write software, model failures, test materials, reject defective solutions and investigate incidents. Reality does not respond to the owner’s charisma.

A rocket does not fly because Musk is confident. It flies because somewhere his confidence was translated into calculations, constraints and tests, and somewhere else it was discarded.

## Huang: natural language does not abolish programming

Jensen Huang has argued that the industry should create technology that makes programming unnecessary and turns human language into the programming language of the future.

It is an effective sentence and a poor account of engineering.

Programming has never been reducible to typing Python, C++ or assembly. Its core is the precise specification of behavior under incomplete information and conflicting constraints.

Even when a person describes a task in natural language, someone must still determine what survives a state transition, which failures are acceptable, what happens on retry, where the trust boundary lies, how a failure is reproduced, who owns a side effect and which test distinguishes a working system from a persuasive imitation.

Syntax can be hidden. Causality cannot.

In August 2026, Huang told investors that a company of roughly 40,000 employees might one day have 400,000 and then four million agents. In October he generalized the promise: every European company would become an AI company and everyone would be able to build agents.

But “agent” is not a unit of labor. A script, ten parallel processes and a thousand short-lived instances may be counted as one agent or a thousand. Without definitions of autonomy, task duration, verification cost, failure rate and human supervision, four million agents does not describe productivity. It describes a convenient way to turn compute processes into an imaginary workforce.

NVIDIA deserves respect for decades of work by architects, CUDA developers, compiler and library engineers, hardware specialists, manufacturing teams, network engineers, cooling experts, quality teams and support staff. It does not need a fictional unit of digital employment to be an extraordinary company.

## Gates: a model demonstration is not an institution

Bill Gates describes a near future in which AI makes good medical advice and high-quality education widely available at almost no cost. In public summaries, this often becomes the claim that humans will not be needed for most tasks.

At least four different problems are being collapsed.

A model may answer an examination question correctly. That does not prove it can manage a patient with incomplete data.

A system may write a strong educational explanation. That does not prove it will discover why a particular child stopped understanding the subject.

The low cost of generating an answer does not imply a low cost of verification, responsibility or repair.

Access to advice is not access to medicine or education as an institution.

In his 2026 forecast, Gates made an even stronger claim: there is no upper limit to how intelligent AI systems will become, and progress will not plateau before they exceed human levels. In August, he wrote that AI can replace and exceed human cognition and that many jobs will disappear forever.

The phrase “no upper limit” is especially revealing. It is not an inference licensed by observation. A finite history of several model families cannot establish an unlimited trajectory of capability. Neither intelligence nor the human level has been defined as a single quantity, and no condition for identifying a plateau has been supplied. This is a metaphysical belief formatted as a technical estimate.

Next to it stands a much more intelligent system. Foundation researchers measure specific diseases and interventions. Teachers test specific programs. Public-health practitioners work with specific national constraints. The closer the work gets to reality, the fewer infinite axes of intelligence it contains and the more conditions, samples, errors and obligations it must carry.

## Dennett: competence does not require an understanding master

Daniel Dennett used the phrase *competence without comprehension*.

Evolution produces functional structures without a central designer who understands the whole. A bacterium solves a problem without formulating a theory. Complex behavior emerges from many local mechanisms.

A technology company works in a comparable way.

No one understands it completely: not an engineer, an architect, a director or a CEO. Competence arises from interacting specializations, procedures, tools, accumulated knowledge and feedback from reality.

Dennett helps explain not the leader’s genius, but its dispensability.

OpenAI can build strong models even if Altman cannot provide a testable definition of AGI. Tesla can manufacture vehicles despite Musk’s failed forecasts. NVIDIA can build exceptional computing platforms even when Huang gives a simplistic account of programming. Microsoft can remain a sophisticated engineering system regardless of Gates’s next generalization about labor.

Companies are not competent because the person at the top understands everything. They remain competent while errors from the top can still be processed below.

## Hurley: an acting system has no single center

Susan Hurley criticized the classical “sandwich model” of mind. In that picture, perception supplies input, central cognition makes a decision and action executes the finished command. Intelligence resides in the middle, separated from world and movement.

Hurley instead treated perception and action as interdependent processes in dynamic feedback loops. In her shared-circuits model, control, predicted feedback, simulation and correction are layered together. Action is not a stupid output produced after intelligence has finished. It helps constitute what the system can perceive and know.

The myth of the great CEO is the corporate version of the old sandwich.

At the top supposedly sits the consciousness of the company. It sees the market, understands the future and makes decisions. Below it are organs of execution: engineers, researchers, operators, manufacturing and support. Their task is merely to materialize the owner’s thought.

Real technology organizations do not work this way. A requirement changes after a prototype. An architecture changes after measurement. A plan changes after a supplier fails. A model changes after red-team evaluation. A release changes after an incident. Every act of execution returns information and restructures the next decision.

Organizational intelligence does not descend from the top. It circulates through loops.

The CEO is one node in that system, albeit a powerful one. He may set goals, allocate capital, choose people and destroy constraints. He is not the place where the company thinks. Sometimes his public speech is worse than the knowledge already distributed throughout his organization.

Hurley completes Dennett. Dennett explains how competence can exist without complete comprehension. Hurley shows why comprehension and control need not live in a single center. Together they offer a better model of the technology company: not a pyramid animated by thought at its peak, but a network of specialized circuits that corrects itself through action.

## Praise the people who cannot replace verification with speech

I do not want to praise these men for vision. Their wealth, authority and media presence already exceed the demonstrated volume of their personal contribution.

Praise the researcher who refused to massage a metric.

The engineer who demanded a reproducible test.

The architect who found the broken invariant.

The quality specialist who returned an expensive project for rework.

The technical writer who preserved knowledge after its author left.

The operator who noticed the weak signal before an incident.

The manager who did not punish a team for delivering bad news.

The doctor and teacher who saw what was absent from the model’s prompt.

These people create reliability. They carry knowledge. They prevent a company from becoming the material embodiment of its owner’s speech.

## Epilogue: give the memory to the data center

At the end of October 2026, Altman supplied an almost perfect final illustration.

While discussing local model inference, he called it “quite terrible” from a global-allocation perspective that a substantial part of the world’s limited DRAM supply sits in personal computers and, by his estimate, is used only five percent of the time. A data center, he argued, would utilize the same memory more efficiently.

He did not literally ask people to pull RAM modules from their machines and mail them to OpenAI. The real argument is more interesting than that caricature.

Memory inside a personal computer becomes not part of its owner’s autonomy, but an inefficiently allocated global resource. The ability to run your own software, keep working state local, withhold data from a cloud provider, operate without a network and control purchased equipment disappears from the calculation. Only utilization remains.

Yet the personal computer emerged precisely as a deliberately “inefficient” alternative to centralized computing power. Its value is not that every byte remains busy around the clock. Its value is that its owner decides when, why and for whom that byte will be used.

A spare room is not occupied one hundred percent of the time. That does not mean it should be transferred to a hotel chain. A private library is closed for most of the day. That does not make it a global paper-allocation error. Reserve capacity, privacy, availability and control look wasteful only in a model where the owner’s rights do not count as useful output.

Here the entire argument of this essay condenses into one sentence. A company built on the concentration of data, compute and capital encounters a memory shortage. Its leader looks at memory owned by individuals and sees not the boundary of someone else’s autonomy, but underutilized infrastructure.

First we were promised that AI would become a personal tool for everyone.

Now the personal computer is apparently an inefficient place for its own memory.
